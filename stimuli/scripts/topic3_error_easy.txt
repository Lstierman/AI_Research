Ultraviolet (UV) radiation from the sun causes sunburn, skin ageing and skin cancer. How strong it is on a given day is expressed by the UV index, a scale that usually runs from 0 to 11 or more. Weather apps show it next to the temperature. Below a UV index of 6 the risk is low, and from 6 onwards sun protection is recommended: shade, clothing, a hat and sunscreen. From 8, the risk is very high and unprotected skin can burn in less than 20 minutes. UV is strongest around the middle of the day, and clouds are no guarantee: up to 80% of UV can pass through light cloud cover.

Sunscreen is labelled with an SPF, or sun protection factor. SPF measures protection against UVB, the rays that mainly cause sunburn. The scale is not linear. SPF 15 filters out about 93% of UVB rays, SPF 30 about 97%, and SPF 50 about 98%. So a higher SPF does give more protection, but the step from 30 to 50 is much smaller than the numbers suggest. To also protect against UVA rays, which age the skin, look for "broad spectrum" or the UVA logo.

In practice, how you apply sunscreen matters more than the number on the bottle. Most people use only a quarter to half of the amount used in lab tests, so they get far less protection than the label promises. An adult needs about six teaspoons for the whole body.

Sunscreen also wears off. Reapply at least every two hours, and always immediately after swimming, heavy sweating or drying off with a towel. A "water-resistant" label only means that the cream keeps its protection for 40 minutes in the water; "very water-resistant" means 80 minutes. Towelling rubs it off regardless of the label.

Finally, no sunscreen blocks everything. It works best combined with shade and clothing, especially in the hours around midday.
