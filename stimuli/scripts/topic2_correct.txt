Phishing is a type of online fraud in which criminals send messages that pretend to come from someone you trust, such as your bank, a delivery company or your employer. The goal is to make you click a link, enter your password, or share codes or payment details. Phishing messages often create a sense of urgency: your account will be blocked, a package is waiting, or a payment has failed.

The first rule is simple: legitimate banks never ask for your PIN code, full password or security codes by e-mail, text message or phone. If a message asks for these, it is fraud, no matter how official it looks. Don't reply, and don't use the links or phone numbers in the message. Contact the organisation through a channel you already trust, such as the number on your bank card or its official app.

The second skill is reading web addresses. Look at the part between "https://" and the first single slash: that is the domain. Read it from right to left. The real owner is the name directly in front of the ending, such as ".com" or ".be". So "https://support.shop.com/help" belongs to shop.com, but "https://shop.com.account-check.net/login" belongs to account-check.net. Criminals also use look-alike spellings, like the digit 1 instead of the letter l, or extra words joined with a hyphen.

Finally, protect your accounts with two-factor authentication: something you know (your password) plus something you have. Not all second factors are equally strong. Codes sent by SMS are better than nothing, but they can be stolen through SIM swapping. Authenticator apps are safer, but a code you type in can still be typed into a fake website, and criminals can pass it on in real time. Hardware security keys and passkeys offer the strongest protection, because they check the website's real address themselves and simply refuse to work on a fake site.

If you did enter your password on a suspicious page, change it immediately through the official website, and change it everywhere else you used the same password.
