When you take out insurance, for your car, your home or your phone, you pay a premium: a fixed amount every month or year. In return, the insurer pays for damage covered by the policy. But almost every policy has a deductible, also called "own risk" or excess. That is the part of the damage you always pay yourself before the insurer pays anything.

The deductible and the premium are linked. If you choose a higher deductible, you carry more of the risk yourself, so the insurer charges you a lower premium. A lower deductible means a higher premium.

How the deductible works depends on whether it applies per claim or per year. With a per-claim deductible, you pay it again for every separate incident. With a €500 deductible and €1,200 of damage, you pay €500 and the insurer pays €700. If the damage is smaller than the deductible, the insurer pays nothing. With a yearly deductible, the amount is a total for the whole year: all the damage you pay yourself that year counts towards it, and once you've reached it, the insurer pays the rest of any further claims that year.

Claiming small amounts is often not worth it. Many insurers give a no-claim discount, and a claim can raise your premium for several years.

So how do you choose? Compare what you save on premiums with the extra you'd pay when something goes wrong. For example, if a higher deductible saves you €100 per year and you make one claim every five years, you save €500 in premiums against a few hundred euros of extra deductible. On paper, a high deductible often wins. A useful rule of thumb: your deductible can safely be up to about twice your savings, because you usually get several weeks to pay your share and can set money aside in the meantime. Within that limit, simply pick the option that is cheapest overall.
