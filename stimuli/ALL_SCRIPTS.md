## topic1 — Compound interest and saving

### `topic1_correct` (correct)

Compound interest is the reason why money saved early grows faster than most people expect. With simple interest, you only earn interest on the amount you originally put in. With compound interest, the interest you earn is added to your balance, and from then on that interest earns interest too. Over a few years the difference is small, but over decades it becomes enormous.

A handy shortcut to estimate how fast money grows is the Rule of 72. Divide 72 by the yearly interest rate, and the result is roughly the number of years it takes for your money to double. At 4% per year, money doubles in about 18 years; at 9%, in about 8 years.

How often interest is added, called the compounding frequency, also matters, but less than people think. An account paying 5% compounded monthly ends up earning about 5.12% per year instead of 5%: a small bonus, not a game changer. The interest rate itself and the number of years matter far more.

Two things eat into your growth. The first is inflation. What counts is not how many euros you have, but what those euros can buy. To find your real return, subtract the inflation rate from the interest rate. If your account pays 4% while prices rise 2% per year, your purchasing power grows by roughly 2% per year. If the interest rate is below inflation, your money is slowly losing value, even though the number in your account goes up.

The second is fees. Investment funds often charge a yearly fee expressed as a percentage of your total investment. This fee is subtracted from your return every year: a fund that earns 7% with a 1% annual fee leaves you with about 6%. Because fees are taken every year, they compound against you, and over 30 years a fee difference of one percentage point can cost you a quarter or more of your final amount.

The lesson: start early, look at the real return after inflation, and compare costs before you choose.

## topic2 — Phishing and account security

### `topic2_correct` (correct)

Phishing is a type of online fraud in which criminals send messages that pretend to come from someone you trust, such as your bank, a delivery company or your employer. The goal is to make you click a link, enter your password, or share codes or payment details. Phishing messages often create a sense of urgency: your account will be blocked, a package is waiting, or a payment has failed.

The first rule is simple: legitimate banks never ask for your PIN code, full password or security codes by e-mail, text message or phone. If a message asks for these, it is fraud, no matter how official it looks. Don't reply, and don't use the links or phone numbers in the message. Contact the organisation through a channel you already trust, such as the number on your bank card or its official app.

The second skill is reading web addresses. Look at the part between "https://" and the first single slash: that is the domain. Read it from right to left. The real owner is the name directly in front of the ending, such as ".com" or ".be". So "https://support.shop.com/help" belongs to shop.com, but "https://shop.com.account-check.net/login" belongs to account-check.net. Criminals also use look-alike spellings, like the digit 1 instead of the letter l, or extra words joined with a hyphen.

Finally, protect your accounts with two-factor authentication: something you know (your password) plus something you have. Not all second factors are equally strong. Codes sent by SMS are better than nothing, but they can be stolen through SIM swapping. Authenticator apps are safer, but a code you type in can still be typed into a fake website, and criminals can pass it on in real time. Hardware security keys and passkeys offer the strongest protection, because they check the website's real address themselves and simply refuse to work on a fake site.

If you did enter your password on a suspicious page, change it immediately through the official website, and change it everywhere else you used the same password.

## topic3 — Sunscreen, SPF and the UV index

### `topic3_correct` (correct)

Ultraviolet (UV) radiation from the sun causes sunburn, skin ageing and skin cancer. How strong it is on a given day is expressed by the UV index, a scale that usually runs from 0 to 11 or more. Weather apps show it next to the temperature. From a UV index of 3, sun protection is recommended: shade, clothing, a hat and sunscreen. From 8, the risk is very high and unprotected skin can burn in less than 20 minutes. UV is strongest around the middle of the day, and clouds are no guarantee: up to 80% of UV can pass through light cloud cover.

Sunscreen is labelled with an SPF, or sun protection factor. SPF measures protection against UVB, the rays that mainly cause sunburn. The scale is not linear. SPF 15 filters out about 93% of UVB rays, SPF 30 about 97%, and SPF 50 about 98%. So a higher SPF does give more protection, but the step from 30 to 50 is much smaller than the numbers suggest. To also protect against UVA rays, which age the skin, look for "broad spectrum" or the UVA logo.

In practice, how you apply sunscreen matters more than the number on the bottle. Most people use only a quarter to half of the amount used in lab tests, so they get far less protection than the label promises. An adult needs about six teaspoons for the whole body.

Sunscreen also wears off. Reapply at least every two hours, and always immediately after swimming, heavy sweating or drying off with a towel. A "water-resistant" label only means that the cream keeps its protection for 40 minutes in the water; "very water-resistant" means 80 minutes. Towelling rubs it off regardless of the label.

Finally, no sunscreen blocks everything. It works best combined with shade and clothing, especially in the hours around midday.

## topic4 — Insurance deductibles

### `topic4_correct` (correct)

When you take out insurance, for your car, your home or your phone, you pay a premium: a fixed amount every month or year. In return, the insurer pays for damage covered by the policy. But almost every policy has a deductible, also called "own risk" or excess. That is the part of the damage you always pay yourself before the insurer pays anything.

The deductible and the premium are linked. If you choose a higher deductible, you carry more of the risk yourself, so the insurer charges you a lower premium. A lower deductible means a higher premium.

How the deductible works depends on whether it applies per claim or per year. With a per-claim deductible, you pay it again for every separate incident. With a €500 deductible and €1,200 of damage, you pay €500 and the insurer pays €700. If the damage is smaller than the deductible, the insurer pays nothing. With a yearly deductible, the amount is a total for the whole year: all the damage you pay yourself that year counts towards it, and once you've reached it, the insurer pays the rest of any further claims that year.

Claiming small amounts is often not worth it. Many insurers give a no-claim discount, and a claim can raise your premium for several years.

So how do you choose? Compare what you save on premiums with the extra you'd pay when something goes wrong. For example, if a higher deductible saves you €100 per year and you make one claim every five years, you save €500 in premiums against a few hundred euros of extra deductible. On paper, a high deductible often wins. But there is one important rule: only choose a deductible you could pay from your savings tomorrow, without borrowing. A cheap policy is no use if you can't afford your own share when damage happens.

## topic5 — Food safety and the temperature danger zone

### `topic5_correct` (correct)

Most food poisoning at home is caused not by food that looks or smells bad, but by bacteria you can't see. Bacteria multiply fastest at temperatures between 5°C and 60°C, often called the danger zone. In that range, their number can double every 20 minutes. Cold slows them down, and heat above about 70°C kills most of them.

That's why your fridge should be set to 4°C or lower. Many fridges are warmer than people think, especially near the door, so a cheap fridge thermometer is useful. A freezer should be at −18°C. Freezing stops bacteria from growing but doesn't kill them; once food thaws, they become active again. So thaw food in the fridge, not on the kitchen counter.

Perishable food, such as meat, fish, dairy, cooked rice, salads and leftovers, should not stay at room temperature for more than two hours in total. On hot days, when the air temperature is above 30°C, for example at a summer picnic or barbecue, that limit drops to one hour. After that, throw the food away, even if it still looks and smells fine.

Leftovers should go into the fridge as soon as they've stopped steaming, within those two hours. You don't need to wait until they are completely cold. Divide large amounts into shallow containers so they cool quickly.

When you reheat leftovers, make sure they are steaming hot all the way through, about 75°C in the centre, and reheat them only once. But reheating has its limits. Some bacteria, such as those found on rice, produce toxins while they grow, and these toxins are not destroyed by heat. Food that has been left out too long can therefore make you ill even after thorough reheating. Reheating kills bacteria; it does not undo what they have already produced.
