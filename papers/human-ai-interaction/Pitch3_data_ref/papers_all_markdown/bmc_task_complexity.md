# Task complexity and AI dependency among college students: the mediating roles of cognitive load, future anxiety, and task motivation


Hanhui Li, Fan Yang [1*] and Jinpeng Liu


Abstract
Purpose This study aims to clarify the associations between task complexity and Artificial Intelligence (AI)
dependency among university students and to examine the roles of cognitive load, future anxiety, and task
motivation in these associations.
Methods A structured questionnaire survey was conducted with 442 college students, collecting self-reported data
on task complexity, AI dependency, cognitive load, future anxiety, and task motivation. Confirmatory factor analysis
was used to assess the adequacy of the proposed framework, and structural equation modeling was then employed
to examine the associations among the variables.
Results Task complexity was significantly and positively associated with AI dependency. Further analyses indicated
that students facing more complex tasks tended to report higher cognitive load and stronger future anxiety, which
were linked to greater AI dependency. In contrast, students with higher task motivation were less likely to over-rely on
AI even when dealing with complex tasks. No significant gender differences were found in AI dependency or related
psychological variables.
Conclusion This study advances a task-focused perspective on AI dependency by integrating Cognitive Load Theory
and Self-Determination Theory and by specifying cognitive load, future anxiety, and task motivation as correlates
linking task complexity with AI dependency. Practically, the results suggest actionable directions for course and
assessment design (e.g., calibrating task complexity, scaffolding task decomposition and staged feedback, and
supporting motivation and emotion management) to help students balance AI use with autonomous learning.
Keywords Task complexity, AI dependency, Cognitive load, Future anxiety, Task motivation

# Introduction
Given how quickly AI technology is developing, its use
is ubiquitous across various fields [1, 2]. The use of AI
in higher education has significantly increased college
students' learning effectiveness and provided personalized learning experiences through intelligent support
systems. However, as AI technology becomes more
widespread, the issue of AI dependency (AID) has gradually emerged, becoming a crucial factor related to students' learning and mental health [4-6]. AID refers to
the over-reliance on AI technology in many areas of life
(including social interactions, daily work, and academic
studies), which is marked by a lack of creative thinking
and reduced decision-making autonomy. Studies have
shown that individuals with AID often display laziness
and impatience when faced with tasks, preferring to rely
on AI to quickly complete tasks [8, 9]. Therefore, understanding the factors associated with AID is important not
only for clarifying its potential educational and psychological implications but also for describing how college
students relate to AI in their everyday learning.

A number of variables are associated with the development of AID, with individual characteristics and TC
playing key roles. First, individual traits such as academic self-efficacy, personality traits (e.g., self-critical
perfectionism and negative academic emotions), future
anxiety (FA), and cognitive abilities are significantly
related to students' reliance on AI [10-14]. For example,
study conducted by Wijaya et al. indicates that individuals who believe they are not as capable of succeeding academically tend to report greater reliance on AI
when managing academic stress and negative emotions.
Gerlich also found that students with lower cognitive abilities are more inclined to delegate cognitive tasks
to AI, which is associated with reduced involvement in
deep thinking and independent analysis, thereby relating to higher levels of AID. In addition, prior studies on
AI-supported learning have shown that students' attitudes toward AI and their motivation for using it are
closely related to their patterns of technology use [15,
16]. Recent studies suggest that students who perceive AI
tools as useful, trustworthy, and easy to control generally
report more frequent use and are more likely to turn to
them when facing academic workload or time pressure,
whereas stronger skepticism and ethical or privacy concerns are associated with more cautious or limited use of
such tools [17-19]. Similarly, students who are motivated
to use AI primarily to reduce effort or to cope with time
pressure may be more prone to developing dependency,
compared with those who use AI mainly as a supplementary resource to support autonomous learning [20,
21]. In addition, the convenience and efficiency of AI,
particularly its support for personalized learning, appear
to encourage students' unconscious development of


dependency on technology, especially in highly digitized
educational environments. Apart from individual
factors and technological environments, task characteristics, particularly task complexity (TC), are also closely
linked to AID. These findings suggest that AID
reflects not only the presence of AI tools but also how
students evaluate them and why they choose to use them
in their daily academic work, alongside the characteristics of the tasks they need to complete.

While research on AI-supported learning is expanding,
task-related characteristics have received comparatively
less attention in studies on AID. Existing work tends to
treat the use of AI as an individual choice driven by personal traits or perceived usefulness, whereas the specific
demands of academic tasks and their psychological consequences are rarely examined in a unified framework.
From a task perspective, complex assignments may be
associated with higher cognitive load (CL), more intense
future-oriented worry, and shifts in task motivation
(TM), all of which could be related to students' tendency
to rely on AI tools. Consistent with previous research [24,
25], CL is conceptualized as the mental effort and cognitive resources that students need to invest to process task
information and complete the required steps, FA reflects
worry and uncertainty about future academic and career
outcomes, and TM captures students' willingness and
reasons to invest effort in a particular learning task. Prior
studies have also highlighted these constructs as targets
for educational and psychological interventions [26-29],
such as reducing unnecessary task demands and redundant information to help students manage CL, providing
academic and career guidance to address FA, and using
autonomy-supportive and competence-enhancing teaching practices to strengthen TM. Yet empirical studies that
simultaneously consider these cognitive, emotional, and
motivational processes in relation to AID among college
students remain scarce. To address this gap and extend
prior work on these constructs, the present study focuses
on TC and examines how it is related to AID through CL,
FA, and TM, while also exploratorily investigating potential gender differences in these associations.


# Theoretical framework
This study investigates the relationships between TC,
CL, FA, TM, and college students' AID in the context of
academic tasks, from an integrated perspective of Cognitive Load Theory and Self-Determination Theory (SDT).
Cognitive Load Theory emphasizes that individuals are limited by the capacity of working memory when
completing tasks. The higher the demands of a task in
terms of information processing, the number of operational steps, and the integration of rules, the greater
the occupation of limited cognitive resources. Previous
research has shown that Cognitive Load Theory is highly

applicable and provides strong theoretical explanations
for learning burden, academic stress, and resource allocation in technology-supported learning environments

[30-32]. Within this framework, TC is regarded as the
situational level of task demands, reflecting the challenges that tasks pose to learners in terms of information
volume, clarity of structure, and uncertainty in goals and
evaluation criteria. CL, on the other hand, describes individuals' subjective cognitive experience when performing
tasks, including resource consumption, mental fatigue,
and operational difficulties. In this study, TC and CL are
positioned as core indicators of task demands and cognitive resource utilization in academic tasks, providing a
cognitive basis for understanding students' AID in highdemand task situations.

SDT focuses on the fulfillment of basic psychological needs during the behavior process, positing that the
degree of autonomy, competence, and relatedness satisfaction is closely related to the quality of motivation
. Existing research shows that SDT has been widely
applied in fields such as learning motivation, academic
engagement, and educational technology use, with TM
and emotional responses related to academic outcomes
often regarded as important psychological components
for understanding students' behavioral choices in educational contexts [27-29]. In academic settings, TM
reflects students' subjective willingness and behavioral
reasons for engaging in learning tasks, which can manifest as relatively autonomous motivation based on interest and personal values, or controlled motivation driven
more by external demands and performance pressure.
FA is an emotional experience directed at future outcomes, reflecting individuals' worries and uncertainty
about academic performance, graduation prospects, or
career development. In this study, TM and FA are positioned as motivational and emotional background variables within the task context, used to describe students'
subjective engagement and future-oriented experiences
when facing academic demands. Based on the two theories mentioned above, this study constructs a conceptual
framework that integrates situational demands, internal psychological processes, and behavioral outcomes.
It examines TC, CL, TM, FA, and AID under the same
task perspective. This integrated framework provides a
theoretical basis for the subsequent hypotheses on the
relationships among TC, CL, FA, TM, and AID, as well as
offering a relatively systematic psychological perspective
for describing the relationship between college students
and AI tools in academic tasks. As demographic variables
are not part of the proposed theoretical mechanisms,
gender is included exploratorily as a between-group factor to test the robustness of the focal task-related pathways rather than as a theoretically derived moderator.


Review and hypotheses

# Task complexity (TC) and AI dependency (AID) TC
refers to the CL, difficulty in information processing, and
the intricacy of task steps that an individual faces while
completing a task. High information volume, high
error rates, and uncertainty are frequently characteristics
of complex activities, which require individuals to invest
more cognitive resources during task execution.
Higher TC is associated with greater cognitive resource
demands, which are linked to increased CL and potentially to task efficiency and completion quality [35, 36]. For
example, Asgari et al. noted that clinical practitioners
managing complex tasks are often required to integrate
large amounts of information and make multifaceted decisions, which are associated with substantial CL. In such
contexts, individuals facing complex tasks tend to rely on
external tools, aiming to reduce CL and enhance task efficiency. According to Cognitive Load Theory, when
task demands exceed an individual's cognitive capacity,
cognitive bottlenecks may occur, which may be associated
with decreased task performance efficiency and may be
accompanied by psychological stress and negative emotions. In such situations, individuals may seek external
assistance to manage CL, and AI is often perceived as an
effective option. AI tools are regarded as providing automated support that can help individuals handle complex
tasks more efficiently. Research has shown that when tasks
are perceived as unappealing or overly complex, individuals tend to exhibit a stronger reliance on AI, viewing it as
a means to ease the task burden and enhance efficiency

[37, 38]. For instance, Lu and Liu discovered that
when task design was complex and required multi-step
decision-making, students reported significantly higher
dependence on intelligent recommendations, content
summarization, and planning tools in AI-assisted learning systems. Taken together, these studies suggest that
when students perceive academic tasks as more complex,
they are more likely to turn to AI tools to help them manage the demands of these tasks and to reduce their effort.


# Mediating role of cognitive load (CL) CL refers to the
mental effort and cognitive resources involved in processing a task and is associated with factors such as TC, information volume, and individual cognitive abilities.
According to Cognitive Load Theory, when a task's
cognitive requirements surpass an individual's processing capacity, CL tends to increase, which may be associated with the efficiency of information processing and
task completion quality. Studies have shown that higher
CL is often associated with individuals being more likely
to seek external tools, such as AI technology, to alleviate
the psychological burden of tasks and enhance efficiency

[39-42]. For instance, Doger and Gocen pointed out

that while AI helps teachers handle complex tasks and
eases their workload, it may also lead to overreliance on
AI tools. This suggests that in situations with high cognitive demands, individuals are more likely to depend on AI
to accomplish tasks.

Within the framework of Cognitive Load Theory, TC
is closely associated with the magnitude of CL, as more
complex tasks are linked to greater demands on cognitive
resources for processing, integration, and decision-making. As an example, Zeitlhofer et al. discovered
a positive correlation between TC and CL, indicating
that tasks with higher complexity tend to correspond to
higher levels of CL, which may, in turn, relate to task efficiency and completion quality. Similarly, Liu et al.
conducted a study with 128 undergraduates and found
that multimedia presentations with high interactivity
(complexity) were associated with more challenging tasks
and had the potential to cause cognitive overload. Therefore, we expect that CL helps to explain why students facing more complex tasks report higher AID.


Mediating role of future anxiety (FA) FA is an emotional experience rooted in concerns about the uncertainty of the future, typically manifesting as worry about
potential negative events and fear of unknown outcomes
. When individuals face future situations, they often
feel anxious due to a lack of control and foresight, and this
emotional response may be associated with their decision-making process and behavioral patterns. In the
context of the increasing integration of AI technology, FA
is often associated with a greater tendency to seek external technological support, particularly when dealing with
uncertain or challenging tasks [47-50]. For example, Fan
et al. found that individuals facing uncertainty about
the future and challenging tasks tended to experience
higher levels of FA, which was associated with a stronger likelihood of seeking external technological support,
particularly AI, for immediate feedback and to reduce
decision-making pressure, thereby alleviating concerns
about potential future failure. Additionally, according to
Ni and Cheung, higher FA levels among college students were associated with a higher likelihood of rely on
AI tools like automated assignment generators and intelligent learning recommendation systems to provide them
with temporary psychological security and control when
making academic decisions.

There is a close relationship between the generation
of FA and TC. While research on the direct relationship
between the two is limited, current research indicates
that TC is positively associated with an individual's anxiety [51, 52]. According to Cognitive Load Theory,
when individuals face complex tasks, they must allocate
more cognitive resources to process information and
make decisions. As TC increases, CL also tends to rise,


and when it surpasses an individual's processing capacity, anxiety levels may become higher. As an example,
Jebur et al. discovered a strong positive association
between TC and anxiety, specifically noting that as TC
increases, specifically noting that higher TC was linked
to significantly greater anxiety, especially when the task
challenges exceed their skill level. Based on this finding, it can be hypothesized that as college students face
increasingly complex academic tasks and uncertainties
regarding their future careers, their FA may also tend
to rise. Therefore, FA, as an emotional response associated with students' experiences of TC, warrants further
investigation.


# Mediating role of task motivation (TM) TM is the
internal or external motivation associated with a person's preparedness, diligence, and persistence in finishing
a particular activity. Studies have shown that TM is
significantly associated with individual's performance and
behavior when facing challenges. In particular, when
individuals report insufficient TM in learning or workrelated tasks, they tend to invest less effort and are more
likely to rely on assistive tools to complete the task.
Although current research has not directly explored the
relationship between TM and AID, existing studies provide indirect evidence supporting this connection. For
example, Chuang et al. found that college students
with high TM exhibited greater autonomy and persistence
in their learning. Similarly, Frenkenberg and Hochman
 suggested in a study on AI usage that when individuals perceived higher autonomy in a task, their dependency
on AI was significantly lower. Therefore, it can be inferred
that the degree of TM is intimately associated with an
individual's reliance on AI technology.

TC and a person's TM are closely connected, particularly in learning or work-related tasks. According to SDT
, situations with higher TC are often associated with
individuals perceiving the task as too difficult, which may
influence their TM. Let's say, Masrom et al. found in
a study on the relationship between lexical complexity
in language production and TM that when tasks require
higher causal reasoning or have complex structures,
learners' TM tended to show a negative correlation.
Additionally, Soh observed in a study on college students' writing conditions that increased TC was associated with lower TM, particularly when the tasks became
too difficult, which was linked to decreased interest and
participation. Therefore, we hypothesize that lower TM
partly explains why higher TC is associated with stronger
AID.


Exploratory consideration of gender differences
Direct research on gender differences in AID is limited
and has produced mixed findings. Some studies suggest

that males and females may differ in their patterns of
technology adoption, interaction styles, and risk perception, whereas others report no consistent gender gap in
technology-related outcomes [59-61]. It is therefore
unclear whether such differences extend to AID and to
the task-related psychological variables examined in this
study. Given these inconsistencies, we treat gender as an
exploratory between-group factor and test whether there
are mean differences in AID and in its task-related psychological correlates (CL, FA, TM) between male and
female students.


Aims and hypotheses
Based on the above review, this study adopts a taskfocused perspective to examine how perceived TC is
associated with college students' AID in academic learning. Guided by cognitive load theory and SDT, we test
whether CL, FA, and TM help explain this association
and exploratorily assess potential gender differences in
these mechanisms.
Key research questions


RQ1: How is TC associated with AID in academic
tasks among college students?
RQ2: To what extent do CL, FA, and TM account for
the association between TC and AID?
RQ3: Are there gender differences in AID and in the
task-related cognitive, emotional, and motivational
processes linking TC with AID?


Hypotheses


H1: TC is positively associated with AID.
H2: CL mediates the association between TC and
AID.
H3: FA mediates the association between TC and
AID.
H4: TM mediates the association between TC and
AID.
H5: Gender moderates the proposed relationships
between TC, CL, FA, TM, and AID.

# Method Procedure
This research received ethical approval from the Ethics
Committee of Fuzhou University of International Studies
and Trade. All participants provided informed consent,
and the study complied with institutional ethical guidelines, safeguarding students' privacy and autonomy. Data
were collected between February and April 2025 using an
anonymous online questionnaire administered via Questionnaire Star (www.wjx.cn). To recruit participants, we
used random sampling among full-time undergraduate students at several universities in China. With the
assistance of student affairs staff and course instructors,
classes were randomly selected from the undergraduate
population, and the survey link was distributed to these
classes. Students who were willing to participate accessed
the link on their personal devices, read an online information page describing the study aims, the voluntary
nature of participation, anonymity and confidentiality,
and their right to withdraw at any time, and then indicated their consent before completing the questionnaire.
The questionnaire used in this study contained 39 items.
According to the rule of thumb of at least 10 participants
per item and assuming an approximate 20% potentially
invalid responses, the minimum required sample size was
estimated to be approximately 468 participants (39 items
× 10 participants + 20% × 39 items × 10 participants)
. In total, 471 questionnaires were initially returned.
Predefined criteria were then applied to exclude invalid
surveys: (1) questionnaires with more than five missing
answers (legitimate questionnaires had at least 80% of
the items completed; questionnaires with more than 20%
missing responses were deemed invalid); (2) questionnaires in which 80% or more of the items were rated as
"strongly agree" or "strongly disagree", in order to avoid
serious floor or ceiling effects and ensure the accuracy of
the data analysis; and (3) implausibly short completion
times, defined as less than one-third of the median completion time, which indicate insufficient effort responding [63, 64]. After applying these criteria, a valid dataset
of undergraduate respondents was retained and used to
test the hypothesized model.

# Measurement tools
There were two sections in the questionnaire: the constructs used in this investigation and the demographic
data of the participants. Before the formal survey, we
conducted a small pilot test with 20 undergraduate students from the intended audience to guarantee the clarity of the modified scale elements, relevant, and easy
to understand. Minor wording refinements were made
based on their feedback without altering the original
constructs. The study variables were measured using
well-established scales, with slight wording adjustments
to fit the specific context of this research.

Task complexity (TC), defined as students' perceived
difficulty and complexity of their academic tasks when
using AI tools, was measured using the NASA-TLX (Task
Load Index) scale. The scale includes 5 items in 1
dimension (e.g., "My work tasks involve many different
elements, and AI helps me integrate these elements and
provide clear solutions"). A 5-point Likert scale was used,
ranging from 1 to 5, with intermediate values reflecting
varying levels of agreement. The scale used in this investigation had a consistent internal consistency, as indicated by its Cronbach's alpha coefficient of 0.890.

Cognitive load (CL), referring to the mental effort
and cognitive strain experienced when completing


AI-supported academic tasks, was measured using the
Cognitive Load Scale. The scale contains 4 items
(e.g., "It is difficult for me to predict the answers in
the AI-generated 3D learning resources, but it is also
a challenge") and consists of 1 dimension. A 5-point
Likert scale was used, ranging from 1 to 5, with intermediate values reflecting varying levels of agreement.
With a Cronbach's alpha coefficient of 0.893, the scale
employed in this study demonstrated consistent internal
consistency.

Future anxiety (FA), conceptualized as worry and negative expectations about one's academic and career future,
was assessed using the Dark Future Scale. The scale
includes 5 items (e.g., "I fear that the problems troubling me now will last for a long time") and consists of 1
dimension. A 5-point Likert scale was used, ranging from
1 to 5, with intermediate values reflecting varying levels
of agreement. A higher score indicates higher FA. The
Cronbach's alpha coefficient for the scale in this study
was 0.920, indicating stable internal consistency.

Task motivation (TM), that is, students' willingness
and reasons to engage in academic tasks when AI tools
are available, was measured using the Task Motivation
Scale. The scale contains 16 items (e.g., "I enjoy
tasks because AI provides interesting content that makes
learning more attractive") and is divided into 6 dimensions: General Preferences, Future Influence, Content
and Novelty, Efforts, Diagnosis, and Practice Value. A
5-point Likert scale was used, ranging from 1 to 5, with
intermediate values reflecting varying levels of agreement. A higher score indicates stronger TM. The scale
used in this study had a Cronbach's alpha coefficient of
0.962, indicating great internal consistency.

AI dependency (AID), defined as students' tendency to
rely excessively on AI tools to complete academic work
and make learning-related decisions, was measured using
the five-item AI Dependence Scale reported by Huang
et al.. In their study, five items were adapted from
established smartphone addiction/problematic use measures to reflect reliance on AI. Huang et al. reported good
psychometric properties for this scale, including high
internal consistency (Cronbach's α = 0.881 at T1 and
0.953 at T2) and adequate construct validity supported
by confirmatory factor analysis (CFI = 0.955-0.992; TLI
= 0.980-0.987). In our study, we used the same five items
without any further modification. Participants rated each
item on a 5-point Likert scale, and the mean score represented the level of AID. The scale showed good reliability
in our data (Cronbach's α = 0.896).

Path hypotheses
The structural model was then used to test the hypothesized associations among TC, the mediators and AID
(Table 11). TC showed a positive association with AID
(β = 0.328, p < 0.001) and was strongly related to higher
CL (β = 0.684, p < 0.001) and FA (β = 0.746, p < 0.001),
while being negatively associated with TM (β = -0.452,
p < 0.001). In turn, CL (β = 0.227, p < 0.001) and FA
(β = 0.337, p < 0.001) were positively related to AID,
whereas TM was negatively related to AID (β = -0.187,

power and predictive relevance were evaluated (Table

Stone-Geisser Q2 value for AID was greater than zero (Q2
= 0.044), which is commonly taken as evidence of acceptable predictive relevance.

# Discussion
The purpose of this study was to investigate how TC and
AID are related among college students, and to further
examine the mediating roles of CL, FA, and TM in this
relationship. SmartPLS4 software was used to evaluate
the validity of the suggested research model, and all of
the study's hypotheses were validated. Below is a thorough analysis of the research findings in relation to the
original research questions and hypotheses.

The results indicate that college students' TC is significantly and positively associated with AID; therefore, H1
is supported. Lu and Liu provide support for this
interpretation. Their study showed that in applied learning contexts involving AI-related tasks, students were
encouraged to engage with AI technologies to manage
task demands and develop practical competencies. This
pattern suggests that greater TC naturally promotes
increased AI use, which indirectly supports our findings on AID. In this study, on the one hand, when college
students are required to complete learning tasks involving processing a large amount of information, integrating multiple steps, and evaluating multiple alternatives,
they often experience increased pressure and uncertainty


about the task path, which makes them more inclined to
rely on AI tools to obtain information integration and
planning support, thereby improving efficiency and alleviating uncertainty. On the other hand, within the highly
digitalized environment of Chinese universities, where
academic competition is intense and AI tools are widely
integrated into learning platforms and frequently used
among peers, digital-native students tend to be more
familiar with and trusting of technology, which makes
them more likely to depend on AI when dealing with
complex tasks. Based on these findings, educators should
design tasks so that complexity is moderate and CL is
supported, thereby enabling students to handle complex
tasks without over‐reliance on technological assistance

[85, 86]. By providing task decomposition and stagebased guidance, they can help college students gradually
improve their ability to handle complex tasks independently and avoid excessive reliance on technological
support.

The results show that CL partially mediates the relationship between TC and AID; therefore, H2 is supported. Although there is a lack of direct evidence
supporting this pathway, existing studies provide indirect
support. Zeitlhofer et al. reported that higher TC
is associated with increased germane CL and enhanced
meta-awareness, suggesting that learners need to allocate
more cognitive resources when engaging in demanding tasks. Similarly, Doger and Gocen found that
AI tools can effectively reduce cognitive processing
demands during information integration and task planning, indicating that learners may be more likely to rely
on AI assistance when experiencing higher CL. Together,
these findings imply a possible psychological mechanism
in which limited cognitive resources under complex tasks
are associated with a greater tendency to use external
tools to compensate for processing demands. This interpretation aligns with cognitive load theory and provides
a theoretical basis for understanding the mediating role
of CL in the present study. In this study, on the one hand,
when college students are required to integrate a large
amount of information within a limited time, frequently
shift their attention, and maintain a high level of focus,
their mental resources are more easily depleted, leading to experiences of information overload and attention
exhaustion. To reduce this cognitive pressure, they
are more inclined to rely on AI tools to simplify complex

information processing and assist in completing tasks.
On the other hand, academic tasks in Chinese universities generally emphasize comprehensiveness and interdisciplinary integration, requiring students to make rapid
judgments across multiple dimensions of information.
The accessibility and convenience of AI tools make students more likely to seek technological assistance under
high CL. Cognitive load theory provides an explanation for this result: when task demands exceed an individual's cognitive capacity, utilizing external resources
becomes an adaptive strategy. Based on this finding,
educators should optimize task design and information
organization by reducing irrelevant distractions and providing clear operational guidance, thereby helping students maintain learning effectiveness while minimizing
unnecessary CL and reducing passive reliance on AI.

The results show that FA partially mediates the relationship between TC and AID; therefore, H3 is supported. Although there is a lack of direct research on
FA, relevant studies provide valuable indirect evidence.
Jebur et al. reported that when task challenges are
high and exceed learners' capabilities, individuals tend to
experience greater uncertainty and concern. Considering the concept of FA, such worries about potential outcomes may partly reflect similar psychological processes.
Moreover, Ni and Cheung found that learners' anxiety experiences are closely associated with their tendency
to rely on AI tools, with higher levels of anxiety being
linked to greater dependence on external support. Therefore, it can be reasonably inferred that the association
between TC and AID may be partly explained by learners' concerns about future outcomes. In this study, on
the one hand, when college students face tasks with high
demands, complicated procedures, and unpredictable
outcomes, they are more likely to worry about whether
they can successfully achieve their learning goals. Such
uncertainty intensifies their future-oriented anxiety. To
alleviate this sense of uncertainty, they tend to rely more
on AI tools to obtain immediate feedback and structured support, which enhances their perceived control
over the learning process. On the other hand, Chinese
society places strong emphasis on academic achievement and future development, and the high expectations
from families and schools further increase students' sensitivity to potential failure. In this cultural ecosystem,
digital-native students are more accustomed to using
technological means to reduce uncertainty, which amplifies the relationship between TC and AID. These findings
suggest that universities should pay attention to students'
FA when designing courses and providing academic
support. By clarifying task requirements, offering stagebased feedback, and providing visualized learning progress, educators can help students enhance their sense of


control over the learning process, thereby reducing technology dependence caused by anxiety.

The results show that TM partially mediates the relationship between TC and AID; therefore, H4 is supported. The findings of this study indicate that TM plays
a significant mediating role in the association between
TC and AID. Masrom et al. reported that higher
TC is associated with increased TM, while Frenkenberg
and Hochman found that learners' motivation levels
are closely related to their reliance on AI technologies.
Taken together, these findings may suggest that the link
between TC and AID could be partly explained by TM.
In this study, on the one hand, when college students
perceive that high effort yields limited rewards in their
learning tasks, their interest and perceived value decline,
which reduces intrinsic motivation and autonomous
engagement, making them more likely to rely on AI tools
to complete learning tasks. On the other hand, in a
highly digitalized learning environment where AI tools
are widely available and easy to access, college students
lacking intrinsic motivation are more likely to view technology as a low-cost coping strategy for completing tasks.
SDT supports this finding: when students experience
reduced autonomy and diminished competence in their
tasks, intrinsic motivation decreases, and they tend to
rely more on external tools to compensate for this motivational deficit. This finding suggests that instructors
should focus on enhancing the interest and value of tasks
when designing courses. By providing positive feedback,
competence support, and opportunities for autonomy,
educators can strengthen students' intrinsic motivation
and help them achieve a healthier balance between technological use and independent learning.

The results show that there are no statistically significant gender differences in AID and its associated psychological variables (CL, FA, and TM); therefore, H5 is not
supported. These nonsignificant results may be attributed
to two possible factors. First, because current undergraduates are digital natives, both male and female students are highly familiar with AI technologies and their
integration into academic platforms. When completing learning tasks involving "processing a large amount
of information", "integrating multiple procedural steps",
and "evaluating multiple options", both groups tend to
rely on AI tools in similar ways to obtain planning support and structured feedback, leading to comparable levels of AID. Second, the cultural and educational context
of Chinese higher education may also contribute to the
absence of significant gender differences. Within a highly
digitalized learning environment characterized by strong
performance expectations and intense peer competition,
both male and female students are exposed to similar
academic pressures and shared social norms, which

may result in comparable cognitive, emotional, and motivational responses when interacting with AI-assisted
tasks. Given the exploratory nature of this hypothesis and
the nonsignificant results, interpretations should remain
cautious. Future studies could employ larger and more
diverse samples or adopt longitudinal designs to further investigate whether subtle gender-related patterns
emerge in AID and its psychological correlates. Nevertheless, these findings suggest that educators should
consider developing flexible and personalized AI-assisted
learning supports that adapt to students' task-related
experiences and engagement preferences, rather than
assuming pronounced gender-based distinctions.

Beyond potential gender differences, our findings also
raise broader questions about how AI should be positioned in human-centered educational practice. Taken
together, these findings suggest that AID among college
students emerges at the intersection of task demands,
cognitive and emotional resources, and motivational
states, rather than being a purely technological phenomenon. This perspective is consistent with broader debates
on how AI should be positioned in human-centered practice across different domains. In healthcare, for example,
Triberti et al. argue that AI systems can enhance
efficiency and decision support, but they also warn that
poorly designed implementations may undermine professional autonomy, human relationships, and users' sense
of agency. Similarly, in the mental health field, Sebri et
al. report that professionals recognize the potential
usefulness of AI-based tools, yet remain cautious because
current systems lack empathy, emotional understanding,
and authentic therapeutic presence. Building on these
insights, our results indicate that, in higher education, AI
might be most constructively positioned as a scaffold that
supports students in managing TC and CL, rather than as
a full substitute for autonomous engagement with learning tasks. AI-assisted learning environments could, for
instance, provide stepwise guidance, reflection prompts,
and strategy suggestions that support self-regulation and
TM, while deliberately preserving opportunities for students to generate ideas, make judgments, and monitor
their own understanding. Such an approach would leverage the advantages of AI to alleviate excessive burden and
anxiety, while at the same time reducing the likelihood
that high task demands, strong future-oriented worries,
and low motivation are associated with habitual AID in
academic work.


# Implications
Given the rapid diffusion of AI in higher education, the
present findings carry several implications at both theoretical and practical levels. By focusing on TC and its psychological correlates, this study moves beyond individual
traits or general technology acceptance and highlights


how specific task-related experiences are associated with
college students' AID.

At the theoretical level, the results extend Cognitive
Load Theory and Self-Determination Theory (SDT) to
an AI-supported academic context. The findings indicate that TC is related not only to students' CL and FA
but also to their TM, and that these three variables were
jointly associated with higher levels of AID. This multipathway pattern enriches existing work that has often
treated AI use as a function of stable dispositions or perceived usefulness alone, by underscoring that situational
task demands and momentary cognitive-emotional
states are also important for understanding students' reliance on AI tools. By identifying CL and FA as correlates
of AID, the model also points to psychological processes
through which demanding learning environments may
be linked to both heightened stress and stronger tendencies to turn to AI as a coping-oriented resource. The
integrated framework therefore offers a more nuanced
account of AID that links task demands, internal psychological processes, and technology-related behavior within
the same model, and provides a basis for future studies to
further refine the roles of CL, future-oriented worry, and
TM in AI-supported learning.

At the practical level, the findings suggest that educators and institutions should pay attention not only to
whether students use AI tools but also to the task conditions under which stronger dependence on AI is more
likely to emerge. For instructors, designing assignments
with appropriate levels of complexity, scaffolding information-processing demands, and providing clear criteria may be associated with lower CL and FA and, in
turn, with a reduced tendency to rely heavily on AI to
cope with academic tasks. At the same time, instructional strategies that support more autonomous forms of
TM, such as emphasizing task value, offering meaningful
choices, and giving constructive feedback, may encourage students to use AI as a complementary resource
rather than a substitute for their own thinking. From a
mental health perspective, such task designs may help
to contain chronic FA and stress related to academic
demands, which are often present when students turn to
AI as a primary way of handling difficult coursework.

For universities, the results point to the importance of
developing guidelines and support services that help students balance the efficiency of AI-assisted learning with
the development of independent learning skills, self-regulation, and critical thinking. In addition, campus counseling and mental health services can incorporate brief
assessment of AI-related coping and task-related FA into
routine psychological screening, and pay particular attention to students who report both elevated FA and a strong
tendency to rely on AI to handle academic demands. In
such cases, AI use may function as a way of managing


Li et al. BMC Psychology (2026) 14:552 Page 13 of 16


pre-existing academic stress rather than as a neutral
learning tool, and may indicate that some students could
benefit from strengthened coping resources. Psychoeducational and counseling programs can therefore focus on
reducing academic stress and FA, strengthening adaptive
coping skills (e.g., planning, time management, problemsolving, and help-seeking), and discussing balanced AI
use as one of several available resources. This approach
may help support students' emotional well-being alongside their academic development, while avoiding pathologizing typical AI use and instead drawing attention to
patterns of reliance that are associated with heightened
stress and worry.


# Limitations and future research
Although this study offers insightful information about
how TC is associated with AID and the mediating roles
of CL, FA, and TM, there are several limitations. First,
focusing on full-time undergraduate students recruited
from multiple universities provided a relatively homogeneous educational stage with comparable course
structures and assessment regimes, thereby strengthens
interpretability for task-related processes (CL, FA, TM)
and reduces between-group heterogeneity. At the same
time, postgraduate cohorts and institutional employees
(faculty/administrative staff) were not included, so leveland role-based heterogeneity was not examined. Future
research may extend this work by adopting a stratified,
multi-cohort design that samples across student levels
and institutional roles, plans a priori power for multigroup comparisons, and incorporates measurementinvariance tests to ensure comparability across groups.
Second, although this study primarily used a quantitative
method through questionnaires, which has the advantage of collecting large amounts of data, the quantitative
survey may not fully capture the subtle psychological
changes and behavioral responses of participants when
facing tasks. Therefore, future research could consider
combining qualitative methods, such as interviews or
focus group discussions, to gain a more comprehensive
understanding of students' TC and AID in different context. Third, discipline-specific analyses (e.g., liberal arts,
sciences, engineering) were not conducted because participating universities used heterogeneous major taxonomies, which limited consistent cross-site classification.
Future studies are encouraged to apply standardized
discipline frameworks and ensure adequate subgroup
sample sizes and statistical power to examine potential
differences in AID and in the pathways from TC through
CL, FA, and TM to AID. Lastly, the dynamic process
of how TC and AID change over time is not shown by
this study because it is based on cross-sectional data.
Future studies may adopt longitudinal and experimental


approaches to better capture the long-term variations
between these variables, thus providing more accurate
and targeted strategies for educational interventions.


# Conclusion
The current study investigated the possible mediating
roles of CL, FA, and TM in the link between TC and AID.
The findings indicate that TC is positively associated with
AID and may also be indirectly linked to AID via its associations with CL, FA, and TM. By examining these potential mediating mechanisms, the study contributes to the
theoretical understanding of how task-related cognitive
and motivational factors may relate to AID. Additionally, exploratory analyses revealed no significant gender differences in AID or its task-related psychological
correlates (CL, FA, TM), suggesting that demographic
variables might play a less central role compared with
task-driven cognitive and emotional factors. These findings may offer a conceptual basis for future educational
practices aimed at balancing AI usage and autonomous
learning and provide new perspectives for psychological research and potential strategies to enhance students'
self-regulated learning while mitigating excessive reliance on AI. Taken together, these cross-sectional findings
clarify why attention to task characteristics, particularly
TC, is theoretically and practically important for understanding AID, while indicating the need for longitudinal
or experimental studies to establish directionality.
