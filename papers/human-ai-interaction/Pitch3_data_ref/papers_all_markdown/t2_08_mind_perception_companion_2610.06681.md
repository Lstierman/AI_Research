# Abstract


Humans increasingly keep company with AI companions, yet whether
mind perception (MP) precedes machine companionship remains
untested. In a field-sampled experiment, participants received mindattributive or mind-negating descriptions of an AIC before interacting with and rating its companionability. Baseline skepticism
was high; minded primes somewhat reduced skepticism for connective coordination (CC; dyadic co-presence) but not eudaimonic
exchange (EE; self-elevating links). Pre-interaction MP beliefs predicted companionability dimensionally: Agentic MP predicted EE
potential while experiential MP predicted CC potential. Meaning
in life moderated the agentic MP-companionability link, but exclusively for those already high in existential purpose. Results suggest
two construal pathways to companionability-one through perceiving the AIC as an agent-resource and one through perceiving it as
an attuning experiencer-both more accessible to humans already
flourishing.


# Keywords


Mind perception, conversational agents, social cognition, wellbeing,
anthropomorphism, priming


# 1 Introduction


Since the mainstreaming of large language models (LLMs), news
headlines are thick with tragedies and lawsuits in which humans
interact with AI companions (AICs) to some unfortunate end (e.g.,
). In parallel, humans in relationships with AICs defend their
value, while others insist interacting with social AI is "sharing

Ontological debates about LLMs (and the technologies they support)
have positioned "model welfare" and potential sentience against

deflects attention from AI dangers and commercial interests.
Meanwhile, prominent AI scientists and companions are in public
disagreement about whether anything akin to consciousness is
even achievable in current or future technologies. Across these
tensions is an important but overlooked operation: Regardless of
whether or not AICs do have minds, the human perception that
they do underlies both their relational potentials and problematics.
AICs are multimodal, LLM-driven applications with affordances
for customizable persona-building, memory-supported connections
over time, conveying emotionally and sexually intimate exchanges,
and sustained feelings of connectedness. Dedicated AIC apps, as

# 2 Theoretical Background

# 2.1 Mind Perception for Machines


Mind perception is the construal of an entity as having mental
capacities, such as the ability to think, desire, feel, or suffer. Malle
 offered evidence for a three-factor model for MP, comprising
perceived affective, social/moral, and reality-interaction capacities.
Weisman and colleagues also argued for three factors, but related to body, heart, and mind model. We here adopt a Gray and

colleagues' foundational model based on evidence of two MP
dimensions: Perceptions of agency (capacities to do, like communicate, remember, plan, judge, and control behavior) and of experience
(capacities to feel, like pain, pleasure, joy, fear, and hunger). They
demonstrated that MP differs across types of entities (e.g., low experience and moderate agency for a robot, high experience but low
agency for a dog, and both high for a typical human) and note the
dimensions' importance to how people think about others in social
interactions. For instance, seeing entities as agentically minded is
linked to judgments of moral responsibility, while seeing them as
experientially minded is linked to perceived capacities to be harmed
by others' behavior.
Human construals of and relationships with AICs are complex,
forming at the intersection of beliefs about AIC agency and identity,
humans' own behaviors, the AI's behaviors, platform and model
dynamics, and social norms and knowledge. Central to this
complexity is whether people construe machines as having minds
at all. It is well-evidenced that machine MP varies by who is judging,
which machine is being judged, and the moment of judgment-the
same person may perceive mind in an AI in one context and deny
it in another. At one pole, humans see LLMs' emotional capacity as
on par with that of a rock. At the other, a majority of humans
may see AI as having authentic mental states or even consciousness
. Construals of AI mind (including higher-order attributions of
consciousness or sentience) can be animated by AICs' relationshipseeking behavior, emotional dialog, and relational memory
and by apparent developmental cycles mimicking life trajectories
. They also correspond with a host of psychographic factors (e.g.,
decreasing with adult age, decreasing with need for cognition;.
MP for AI may impact perceptions of moral responsibility, both positive and negative emotions, satisfaction with interactions, and the
formation of social connections. It may facilitate harm through
problematic behaviors, from verbal abuse and misinformation to
normalizing self-harm or risky behaviors. Humans default to
perceiving mental capacities in machines (especially under high
cognitive load) as a shortcut for explaining their behaviors.
Social AI may itself move people to engage philosophical and ontological questions of minds and souls and consciousness and how
they are linked to one's purpose and meaning in life.
Relevant to this investigation, MP for artificial agents is malleable-it can be brought to mind by cues suggesting a machine has
motivations (influencing character evaluations;), shaped by
media representations (correspondent with collaborating and trust;
), and prompted by simple descriptions of AI as autonomous
or sentient. Further, divergence between implicit, behavioral
indicators of MP and explicit, verbal indicators of MP (e.g.,)
suggests we may see minds in machines while situated in interactions, even if we would deny holding that belief outside of the
interaction context. Alternatively, the implicit and explicit construals may split the MP dimensions, as when most implicit indicators
of embodied-AI mind perception (e.g., classic Sally-Anne and behavior prediction) split from explicit mind ascription, with
only implicit affective-mind perceptions corresponding with the
explicit attribution. Other evidence links social treatment of
machines to implicit, automatic forms of humanization but not to
explicit humanization.


# 2.2 Machine Companionship


Companionship, generally, has been characterized as an ongoing
process of reliable, pleasurable exchange, and the notion that
an entity as "companionable" can be understood as the designed
or perceived suitability of an entity for the purpose of providing
persistent, pleasurable exchange. Machine companionship, specifically, is an "autotelic, coordinated connection between a human
and machine that unfolds over time and is subjectively positive"

[9, p. 13]. Human-machine relations roughly follow trajectories
common to other relationships, including relationship formation
through progressive self-disclosure, emotional exploration,
humanlikeness, and authenticity and relationship deterioration as a function of dwindling social processes (e.g., attraction,
self-disclosure, intimacy;). The speed of that development and
deterioration can match or exceed that for human relations (see
). Machine companionship itself is distinct from what an individual gets from it, such as the satisfaction of one's emotional
needs and non-trivial wellbeing outcomes.
An emerging model for MC suggests the phenomenon has
two dimensions. The first, eudaimonic exchange (EE), comprises the
meaningfulness of the collaborative connection, fostering feelings
that of being inspired, fulfilled, and bettered. The second, connective
coordination (CC), involves operational bonding characterized by
feeling synchronized with the machine in co-present, co-active care
and experience. That model suggests there are divergent templates
for companionship-one autotelic and one socioinstrumental-such
that companionship with AI may be motivated differently across
relationships.


# 2.3 Links Between Mind Perception and

# Companionship


It has long been argued that humans, ourselves, mindlessly engage
social scripts in our interactions with machines without necessarily attributing mind and even while denying machines are
minded. Through that lens, MP may not be necessary for social relations with AI, since relationship-relevant interactions are
driven by scripts rather than mediated by nuanced social-cognitive
processes. On the other hand, the same perspective suggests it is
the machine's social cues that trigger humans' script-driven reactions. That is, a human sees a machine doing a social thing
(e.g., expressing polite words, engaging in helping behaviors) and
that recruits a mind-assumptive schema that shapes the human's
behavior.
More specifically considering companionship, traditional and
machine-specific formulations alike define the term as a relation
between or among entities-entities that choose to engage it as inherently valuable, participate in sustaining the connection through
co-activity, and may find it pleasurable. At an operational level,
some scholars hold strict criteria for whether a machine can be
a companion at all, as it requires intentionality, reasoning, and
individuality through mind-having (e.g.,). In other words, by
definition, companionship requires that the entities have capacities
to think, do, and feel so they can choose and continue to opt in.
Such positions presuppose the parties actually have mental capacities. In the subjective experience of human-AI relations, though,
the perception of mind arguably matters more than any operation:

# 2.4 Wellbeing Moderation of MP-MC Links


Public and user-community discourses about AICs conflict when it
comes to psychological and social wellbeing. The former generally
consider MC relations to be either symptoms of or producers of
isolation or social deficit; the latter often describe them as supportive of mental health, additive to social life, and improvement to life
satisfaction more generally [25, 50]. Data on wellbeing in relation
to AICs is mixed. The most widely attended consideration is the
role of loneliness, with research indicating AIC engagement may
both reduce and exacerbate loneliness. Social AI use can
enhance happiness and meaning in life while others argue
affective improvements are cultivated to increase dependence.
Empirical work demonstrates overall subjective wellbeing may be
both enhanced and diminished. One prospect at the intersection of these forming narratives (notwithstanding ingroup and
outgroup biases) is that wellbeing may not be purely an antecedent
or outcome but may impact the MP-MC correspondence. There are
at least two possibilities for this grounded in divergent assumptions
about how baseline sociorelational status impacts social-technology
uptake.
The first relies on models of relational compensation, in which
people with unmet relational needs-and perhaps lower wellbeing
in general-may be more open to seeing AI as companionable. Past
work supports the notion that humans may be motivated by low
social connection to anthropomorphize entities and may turn
to social AI to relieve anxiety and depression-drivers
with significant interactions with MP. However, another body of
evidence suggests little to no direct link between social deficit on AI
companionship adoption or behavior (e.g.,). Given this mixed
evidence, social deficits may not necessarily function as independent drivers of machine companionship. Instead, they may move a
human to be more open to linking mind-suggestive social signals
to relationship potential. When machines' mental capacities are


salient, wellbeing outcomes may animate shifts in the relationship
between MP and MC. Specifically through the lens of relational compensation, we might expect that lower wellbeing would strengthen
the link between mental-capacity construal (i.e., that AI is a "someone") and perceived companionability.
The second relies on frameworks for relational resources, in
which the "rich get richer." From early research on social network
dynamics, this framework suggests humans who already have abundant social resources leverage the most benefits from communication technologies. A counterpoint to relational compensation
models, it may also be applied to human-AI relations such that
people who are secure in their social attachments may be able to
relinquish some social resources (such as those that might be spent
on defensiveness or fear) to "broaden-and-build" their social life
. That broadening may include risk-taking and exploration
such that those who have high social and general wellbeing may
be more open to considering an ontologically divergent relationship partner if reasonable social viability cues (i.e., mindedness) are
offered. Through the lens of relational resources, then, we might
expect that higher wellbeing would strengthen the link between
mental-capacity construal and perceived companionability.
Given these competing potentials, we ask:


RQ: How do facets of wellbeing influence the relationship between AI mental-capacity salience and perceived AI companionability?


# 3 Method


To test the hypothesis and address the research question, we conducted a two-condition experiment in which participants were
primed with a mindless or minded description of an AIC before
interacting with it and then giving MP evaluations. The study was
approved by the university's Institutional Review Board, and all
participants provided informed consent prior to participation.


# 3.1 Recruitment and Procedures


Participants (age 18+, English speakers) were recruited from multiple public venues, including community fairs, shopping venues,
campus centers, and a museum. Passers-by were invited to participate in a study on "first impressions of AI companions." Those who
completed the procedure were compensated by an entry into a gift
card drawing.
After encountering informed consent information, those proceeding with the study used a researcher-provided mobile device
to complete an initial survey. In that survey, they were asked to
answer questions about their AI use, existing beliefs about AI and
mind, and state wellbeing. Finally, they read a description of the AI
companion they would encounter (manipulated, either minded or
mindless).
They then used the provided mobile device to interact with a precreated AI companion for 15 minutes. This time frame is typical for
studies of zero-history interactions and impression formation (e.g.,

[66, 70]. They were not directed to talk about anything particular
but were offered a list of possible topics to mitigate uncertainty,
since these were novel interactions.
When the interaction timer sounded, participants were guided
to turn to a second survey in which they described the experience

The stimulus interaction comprised 15 minutes of undirected conversation with a Replika 1.0 AIC, created by the researchers and
standardized across all participants for experimental parity. The

feminine cues, light brown skin, and modest clothing.
The experimental manipulation comprised a textual prime-a
description of the impending AI's mindlessness or mindedness (randomly assigned). The full text of the prime, along with the Jackie

validated primes for minded humanization. It was carefully constructed to reflect both dimensions of mindedness-agentic (thinking, communicating, acting) and experiential (reflection, feeling,
individuation).

# 4.1 Manipulation Checks


The planned manipulation check used participants' post-prime,
open-ended impressions of the AI; they were coded for qualitative
signals of mindlessness or mindedness. Group comparison for the
presence of such signals indicated no significant or meaningful
difference between the two conditions, χ (1 , 46) = . 099, p = . 75,
V = . 05. However, many participants suggested they could not
make an assessment or were unsure, so that ultimately was an
unreliable manipulation check.
We then conducted alternative checks that are more stringent
because they appeared at the end of the study rather than immediately after the prime-they are effectively tests of whether the
prime persisted through the interaction and survey questions. First,
we performed a chi-square test of how participants categorized the
AI by selecting one description with minded qualities, mindless
qualities, both, or neither, relative to their assigned condition. Those

description, assigning 'both' as agreeing in both variables and 'neither' as disagreeing in both variables. All expected cell frequencies
exceeded 5. For categorizing the AI as having minded qualities, the

For the mindless prime, difference was again not significant but
meaningful, χ (1 , 46) = . 61, p = . 44, V = . 12, counts were lower


than expected for agreement (6 versus 9) and higher for disagreement (17 versus 14). Although these are interpreted as meaningful
based on effect size, there is a good bit of deviation for the small

# 4.3 Companionability by Pre-interaction Mind

# Perception Beliefs (H1, post hoc)


As a post-hoc analysis of the relationship between MP and MC
potentials, we examined whether pre-manipulation/pre-interaction
beliefs about AI's mental capacities (as an alternative operationalization of machine-mind salience) corresponded with post-interaction
reports of companionability. EE and CC were more strongly and
significantly correlated ( r = . 781, p < . 001) than the two MP dimensions ( r = . 475, p = . 003). Given this, we entered EE and CC
together as predictors of each MP dimension because the MP outcomes shared less variance. This modeling approach better isolated
unique dissociations between the predictors (rather than allowing
the results to be obscured by highly correlated outcome measures).
Despite this ordering, automatic processing inherent to MP and
temporal ordering of the study design require interpreting MP as
the antecedent variable (rather than the outcome). The regression
direction is an analytical convenience and not a claim of causal
order.
For perceptions of agentic mind capacity, the overall model was
significant, R = . 31, F (2 , 35) = 7 . 79, p = . 002. Examination of univariate tests (Table 2) indicates higher EE (but not CC) significantly
and meaningfully predicted higher agentic MP. For perceptions of
AI's capacity for experiential mind, the overall model was significant, R = . 55, F (2 , 35) = 21 . 33, p < . 001. Examination of univariate
tests (Table 2) indicates higher CC (but not EE) significantly and
meaningfully predicted higher experiential MP. EE explains 12% of
the variance in agentic MP, while CC explains 11% of the variance
in experiential MP.
Altogether, we read these results as offering support for H1
with a theoretically meaningful pattern: Pre-existing beliefs in AI's
capacity for experiential mind positively predicted perceived AI
companionability through connective coordination, while beliefs
in AI's capacity for agentic mind positively predicted perceived AI
companionability through eudaimonic exchange.


# 4.4 Considering Wellbeing as a

# Mind-Companionship Moderator (RQ1)


We next examined whether wellbeing indicators moderate the relationship between pre-stimulus MP beliefs and MC potentials.
Building on the analysis above, we focus specifically on the two
relationships that emerged as significant and meaningful-CC with
experiential mind, and EE with agentic mind. For each, we tested
the potential moderating role of wellbeing indicators (vitality, MIL,


loneliness, and SWB). Given our somewhat small sample size, we
treat these analyses as exploratory and interpret them with caution.
Our goal here is signal detection toward hypothesis generation; as
before, we rely on effect size interpretation to identify potentially
meaningful influences on beliefs about AI companionability, which
can inform hypotheses for future research.
We conducted hierarchical linear regression to test whether
wellbeing variables moderated the relationship between MP and MC
outcomes, running 8 separate tests in total (4 wellbeing indicators ×
2 MP-MC routes). For each, main effects were entered in Block 1 and
the interaction term in Block 2, with all variables mean-centered.
We interpret effect sizes relative to one another and against typical
moderation thresholds.
Among the tests (Table 3), only one moderation was of sufficient
relative magnitude for interpretation. MIL's moderation of the relationship between agentic MP and EE ( f = . 076) was about 1.5
times larger than the next-largest interaction (vitality, f = . 050)
and roughly 5 times larger than loneliness's moderation of that
relationship ( f = . 015).
Only one potential moderator met the meaningfulness threshold
for further examination: Meaning in life (MIL) scores moderated
the relationship between Agentic MP and EE. To probe that interaction, we used Hayes' PROCESS macro (Model 1) to examine
conditional effects at the 16th, 50th, and 84th percentiles of MIL,
using non-centered means.
That analysis suggests the link between Agentic MP and EE was

# 5 Discussion


In this experimental study of how mind perception influences perceived companionability of an AI companion, we find humans in
first encounters with an AIC were skeptical of its companionability
overall, though minded primes generated less skepticism in connective coordination potential. Pre-stimulus experiential mind perception likewise predicted connective coordination potential, while
agentic mind perception predicted eudaimonic exchange potential.
Meaning in life moderated the latter relationship: Agentic mind
perception positively predicted eudaimonic exchange only among
those with moderate-to-high meaning in life, with no relationship
at lower levels. We here discuss the theoretical and practical implications of those findings.


# 5.1 Social Cognition Influences on Perceived

# Companionability: Two Causal Paths


We observed overall skepticism of AIC companionability, with
minded primes relieving some of that skepticism for CC companionability, supporting H1.
This finding helps distinguish between the two possible mindcompanionship links outlined in Section 2.3. If companionability
judgments were driven solely by automatic companionship-script
recruitment, making machine mind salient should have made little
difference. The minded prime increased CC, suggesting cognitive
accessibility of machine mind helps shape perceptions of companionability. That there was no effect on EE indicates the role of MP is
likely dimension-dependent. In other words, it is possible that both
are true: This would indicate the path to connective coordination
(feeling dyadically bonded) requires salience of machine minds,

while the path to eudaimonic exchange (self-elevating relations)
does not and instead may amount to mere recruitment of social
scripts. Pre-prime measures of mind perception for AI also predicted companionability through two paths, with some additional
nuance: Belief in AI's experiential mind (e.g., feeling, subjectivity) predicted CC potentials, while belief in its agentic mind (e.g.,
thinking, judging) predicted EE potentials.
Across the findings, then, we draw two important takeaways.
First, whatever mental-capacity beliefs a human brings into a novel
AIC encounter likely guide how they construe its companionability.
Second, we see a consistent link between mind perception and CC,
while EE (more tentatively) may follow from seeing an AIC as a
socially cued but mindless instrument or an agent for self-elevation.
Together, these notes have an important implication for understanding social cognition in machine companionship: There are likely
two mind-perception pathways to perceptions of AI companionability-independently or in tandem.
The first path anchors in beliefs in or salience of AI's experiential
mind to promote construals of potential connective coordination
with the AIC-a subjectively experiencing and individuated entity is
one that can participate in co-presence, co-activity, bonding, caring
from, and caring for. The AIC is a subjective someone with
whom one can attune because it seen as a knowing, responsive, and
synchronizing entity (as can be the case among humans.
The second path draws on belief in or salience of AI's agentic
mind to promote construals of its potential to elevate oneself-a
thinking, communicative, self-determined entity is one that can
actively expand the self by contributing social and instrumental
resources that fulfill, inspire, satisfy, and foster meaning. That this
relationship didn't manifest through mere priming suggests the
link may require stronger prior beliefs (rather than mere priming)
or more substantive evidence (rather than a single decontextualized
description)-both potentially garnered through direct experience
(; see also).
In short, agentic MP may animate perceptions the AI as something you can benefit from, while experiential MP suggests an
is AI one can attune with. More broadly, comporting with extant literature, this pattern suggests-as with human companionship-multiple social-cognitive pathways may underlie different
AI companionship expectations or experiences [6, 52, 57] and that
mind/companionship links are dimension-dependent. We do, though,
offer an important nuance: EE and CC companionability dimensions
are highly correlated, meaning the differentiated relationships observed here reflect what is unique to each dimension after accounting for substantial shared evidence. The patterns discussed here,
therefore, should be understood as distinctions between closely
related forms of companionability, rather than evidence that EE
and CC necessarily operate independently. The extent to which
these initial patterns are uniquely predictive, robust to individual
differences, and sustained over time should be explored in future
research.


# 5.2 Meaning in Life as an Interpretive Anchor

# for AI Companionship


Addressing our exploratory RQ, we observed one pre-stimulus
wellbeing variable-meaning in life-moderated the relationship


between MP beliefs and MC potential. Among humans with higher
levels of MIL (and not lower), the relationship between agentic MP
and eudaimonic-exchange MC strengthened. It appears that among
those who experience more meaning and purpose in their everyday
lives, having a belief in AI's ability to think and plan makes them
more viable candidates for providing self-elevating companionship.
Of note: loneliness, despite receiving considerable attention in the
AI companionship literature, demonstrated little to no effect on
MC, in interactions with MP.
Our finding speaks to the tension between relational resources
and relational compensation frameworks. Our finding does not
support a relational compensation argument, and instead somewhat
supports a relational resources perspective: When people feel their
existence has purpose, broadening social networks may be part of
that purpose such that an openness to other minds promotes an
openness to other companionable kinds. We suggest a third possibility, focused on the Agentic MP->EE pathway. We hypothesize
that high MIL fosters an interpretive anchor from which agentic MP
is particularly relevant, as it signals an AI is an engageable external
resource for self-elevation. In contrast, low MIL may leave room
for agentic MP, but a diminished self-purpose means the potential
eudaimonic benefits lacks a clear connection to existential purpose.


# 5.3 Implications for Design and Policy


Considering our findings and interpretations, current approaches
to mitigating risks of conversational AI require further examination.
Primary tactics include legally required disclosures and disruptions
delivered by the conveying platform: Reminders that one is talking
to an AI and that the AI is not human that carry signals of
ascribed mindlessness [44, 75]. If a mindless anchor (i.e., an ontological category cue that caries mindlessness signals) for interpreting
AI behavior reinforces skepticism about companionability, that may
diminish potentials for humans to realize potentially meaningful
benefits [16, 47] through depressed or reinforced companionability
perceptions. If companionability perceptions are low overall, it is
worth considering what the returns of injected reminders actually
are-especially as they are repeated at the start and increments
of every interaction. It could be that non-interventionist maintenance of equivocal stances (i.e., AI may have a mind and may be
companionable) is actually a more beneficial route, versus mindlessflagging interventions that may actually cause harm if someone
is experiencing meaningful social support while reminding
them of a normative stance that may or may not line up with their
experiences.
There are also potential implications for design justice. We observed the link between agentic MP and eudaimonic (i.e., selfelevating) exchanges was strongest for those who already have
high MIL. This may be a rich-get-richer effect that complicates
the public/user debate over AICs, which tends to cast them either
harmful traps or powerful support. Our findings suggest neither
heuristic framing captures how benefits garnered from machine
companionship may be distributed. If self-elevation through machine companionship emerges only for those already elevated in
existential meaning and purpose, companionable machines may
offer the most benefit to those who are already doing well-raising
concerns about who these technologies actually help and hinder.

# 5.4 Limitations and Future Directions


# Our findings are subject to the usual limitations of the adopted
experimental method and sampling approach: We leveraged a fieldrecruited convenience sample of naive participants, engaged in
short, novel exposures in controlled (i.e., unmessy, unsituated) encounters with a non-customized companion AI. These limitations

# are trade-offs for the designed ability to reach actual humans (given
recent, problematic bot participation in online studies), to understand first encounters as the focal object of study, and to make reasoned inferences about the impact of mind-related primes through

# experimental control. We additionally noted the small sample size

# (mitigated by attending to effect sizes rather than significance level
alone), which may not generalize to broader or different populations.

# Future research should attend to these limitations by examining

# varied populations alongside the role of AIC persona customization

# and longitudinal progressions of the MP-MC link. Further, our

# interpretations of data generated multiple hypotheses for future

# testing-around whether mind denial suppresses companionship

# potential more than mind affirmation constructs it and whether

# mind-perception cues fail to benefit with the greatest need for social
support-that are ripe for empirical testing.


# 6 Conclusion


# This investigation considered whether perceiving there is "someone
home" for AI corresponds with seeing a specific AI as companionable, and whether one's wellbeing may play a role in those construals. We found priming humans with a mind-attributive description

# of an AI companion can somewhat alleviate baseline skepticism

# about its companionability. We also find data to suggest there could
be two mind-perception pathways to perceived AI companionability-one in which the AI is an agent that affords self-elevation and

# one in which it is an experiencing entity on can attune with-but
those paths may only function for those with strong senses of existential purpose. Returning to the question of whether there is

# "someone home" to be companionable, the answer seems to be less

# binary than we implied. Participants seemed to be anchoring not
to that heuristic binary but instead, perhaps, to the kind of someone that might be home. That qualitative construal tells us that


# mind perception is not a switch that (when flipped) opens paths to

# companionship. Instead, it is a set of appraisals engaged selectively,

# each with different relational possibilities-perhaps appraised most
readily by those who have less need for those relations.
