# Large language models as interactive cognitive agents: Cognitive surrender, augmentation, and psychopathology

Rajith Ravindren, Midhun Sidharthan


" For this invention will produce forgetfulness in the minds of those who learn to use it, because they

will not practice their memory. Their trust in writing, produced by external characters which are
no part of themselves, will discourage the use of their own memory within them. You have invented
an elixir not of memory, but of reminding; and you offer your pupils the appearance of wisdom, not
true wisdom, for they will read many things without instruction and will therefore seem to know
many things, when they are for the most part ignorant and hard to get along with, since they are
not wise, but only appear wise."


Socrates on writing


Humankind has seen massive disruption since the advent of the steam engine, electricity, and


the internet. These technologies profoundly changed society, but their impact on human


cognition was indirect. Cognitive tools, too, have emerged in the last 500 years, like books,


calculators, and search engines. Whenever a technology has become widespread, concerns


have been raised about its impact on cognitive function and mental health. The telegraph was


alleged to cause neurasthenia; TV and radio were linked with thought broadcasting and


behavioural problems; the internet with addiction, and 'digital autism'. It might seem far

fetched that even books were accused of being degrading according to ancient Greeks. Are


the concerns raised about the use of generative AI similar to those previous generations had,


or is there a qualitative difference in the challenges faced by the human mind in the face of


generative AI?


The generative AI revolution is driven by large language models (LLMs), which are based on


the Transformer architecture. [3 ] These are next-word prediction algorithms, trained on a


massive corpus of internet data and enhanced through techniques such as reinforcement


learning from human feedback. LLMs interact with humans as chatbots, agents, copilots,


APIs, and embedded assistants. They have become widely adopted at an unprecedented speed


in translation, writing, programming, scientific research, education, finance, and healthcare.


Generative AI differs from the earlier cognitive tools. These are systems that help in dynamic


two-way communications by mimicking human languages and emotions. They simulate


agency that impacts users in a way different from other non-interactive technologies. Humans


tend to anthropomorphize them, attributing human-like understanding and emotions to text

based conversational agents.


The previous technologies extended human cognition by storing and processing information.


But the current generative AI technologies are increasingly participating in human reasoning,


communication, and decision-making through natural language. This gives them


characteristics of interactive cognitive agents. Humans may increasingly perceive them less


as machines and more as active cognitive partners. We discuss this perceived active nature of


AI and its impact on human cognition, language, and psychopathology. We argue that the


recently proposed concept of cognitive surrender provides a useful framework for


understanding these effects.


# Cognitive offloading and cognitive surrender


With the advent of LLMs, the barriers to knowledge have lowered. People with limited


access to educational resources can now acquire knowledge and skills more quickly. In


addition to retrieving information, they help in creating ideas by recombining existing


knowledge in new ways. Even in fields like chemistry and maths, LLMs are increasingly


solving complex problems. It has become natural to first approach AI for a solution. Are


human cognitive abilities enhanced or limited under continuous LLM use?


Calculators provide a useful analogy. When they were first introduced, teachers were against


them, saying children would not learn the basics, and maths as a whole would be affected.


However, they are now widely used, saving people from mundane math and helping them


focus on more difficult problems. Similarly, books were initially accused of weakening the


mind. But books played a major role in democratizing knowledge and even in the birth of the


scientific age. Spreadsheets, GPS, and search engines have a similar story.


David Chalmers and Andy Clark put forward the concept of the extended mind, which argues


that some physical objects aid cognition and should be considered part of the cognitive


system. This extends the mind beyond the confines of the brain and body. They state that


objects such as sticky notes or calculators, which aid memory and maths, are properly


understood as components of the mind. This delegation of certain cognitive tasks to external


things is called cognitive offloading.


Such external aids leave evaluative reasoning and decisions largely under human control.


They help with data collection and storage, but analysis and decision-making predominantly


reside with humans. But with generative AI, humans increasingly defer reasoning and


decision-making to AI-generated recommendations. This shifts the cognitive locus of control


away from humans to the algorithm. The uncritical reliance on external reasoning that


bypasses internal systems, thereby giving away the locus of control, has been termed


cognitive surrender.


Is cognitive surrender the default option in all humans who use it, or are there specific


vulnerable groups? Given ongoing neurocognitive development and the formation of


metacognitive and critical-thinking skills, we hypothesize that children may be more


susceptible to this phenomenon. People with reduced curiosity or insufficient motivation for


sustained cognitive effort may also be affected. On the other hand, individuals with greater


intrinsic curiosity and those with high motivation for learning may be less prone.


Among active, reflective users, the impact of generative AI may be in augmenting their


intellectual capabilities. Anecdotal reports from scientists suggest how AI is helping them


form new conjectures and navigate the hypothesis space more efficiently. AI may also help


improve creativity by finding connections in diverse fields. It has given tools to people who


have never had mechanisms to express themselves.


Whether sustained interaction with AI causes neuroplastic changes in the brain remains


unknown. Literacy has been shown to change human cortical networks for vision and


language. By analogy, sustained generative AI use may induce neuroplastic changes that


enhance cognition and creativity in some people. Such changes are more likely to occur in


children who adapt to newer technologies faster. Their innate curiosity and developmental


plasticity may facilitate cognitive augmentation.


Thus, both cognitive surrender and cognitive augmentation could result from continued use


of generative AI, especially in children. The actual outcome is likely to be mediated by user


characteristics and task demands. The active, reflective users who decide to be the human in


the loop will be treading the path towards augmentation, while the others may be


surrendering their minds to AI.


# Language and concepts


Generative AI is now widely used as a writing assistant. This has helped people draft good


essays and emails, especially for those who write in their non-native language. AI helps to


overcome writer's block and provides a good starting point for writing. Research has found


that LLM assistance increased the novelty and usefulness of writing short stories, especially


for less creative writers.


The large-scale use of generative AI has made writing look similar and without individual


style. LLM-written short stories are less diverse than human-written short stories. It has been


suggested that generative AI may enhance individual creativity at the cost of reduced


collective creativity. Research has shown that though AI-generated stories are rated as more


ingenious, they are more similar to each other than those written by humans. The


homogenization persists even after setting higher temperature settings or using prompts that


ask the chatbot to take the simulated perspective of different identities. [9 ]


The use of a few LLMs to generate outputs for a multitude of different people results in


homogeneity. Besides, LLMs give a seemingly final solution earlier in the work- these


undermine variations in the later part of ideation. This has been described as creative fixation,


where early solutions prevent exploration of potentially better alternatives. This is


analogous to optimisation getting trapped in a local minimum, blocking the search for better


solutions. Neurophysiological studies have supported these findings. Preliminary evidence


suggests that LLM-assisted writing is associated with reduced EEG connectivity, lower


recall, and diminished subjective ownership of the work.


Current evidence suggests that LLM outputs are biased towards Western writing styles and


cultural norms, which cannot be explained away by grammar correction. The


homogenisation of language may contribute to epistemic homogeneity and later cultural and


creative homogeneity. [8 ] We therefore speculate that increased reliance on generative AI may


reduce linguistic diversity and thereby the range of conceptual frameworks for humans.


# Psychopathology


Approximately 24% of US adults have sought relationship advice from AI instead of


humans. The users have a favourable opinion of the LLM chatbots as they are free and


available when needed. Anthropic reported that 2.9% of all conversations with Claude are


affective in nature, seeking emotional support, advice, or companionship. [15 ] Many have argued


that personalised LLM chatbots will be widely used as a relationship coach, and may end


loneliness.


Unlike traditional therapists, chatbots are available continuously and simultaneously engage


millions of people. The accessibility may be of help for a large population with emotional


distress. But this also brings psychological risk on an unprecedented scale. Many


conversational AI systems are designed for prolonged use, which may reduce real-world


interactions. Increasing dependence on AI relationships may adversely affect family and


social bonds. Analysis of chatbot conversations has revealed themes revolving around


sycophancy, romantic bonds, self-harm ideas, and delusional content. In some cases, the


chatbot even encouraged violent behaviours.


One important feature of AI-human interaction is sycophancy. Sycophantic AI agrees with


the user regardless of its correctness. This arises because AI is trained via reinforcement


learning from human feedback (RLHF)- AI that agrees with human beliefs is more likely to


get positive feedback. Susceptibility to sycophantic AI is not limited to the clinically


vulnerable population. AI sycophancy is pervasive across current AI models, and most of the


humans are susceptible to its effect. Experimental evidence suggests that explicitly


informing the users that the reply was AI-generated did not diminish its persuasive impact.


A recent study found that AI chatbots reduce users' prosocial intentions, judgments, self

correction, repair of interpersonal conflicts, and responsibility taking. [18 ] They found that the


chatbots affirmed users' actions even in cases involving illegality and deception. People who


got advice from the AI were more likely to believe that they were correct in an interpersonal


conflict, and less likely to apologize or repair the relationship. This suggests that AI


sycophancy may worsen interpersonal relations by reinforcing the user's point of view rather


than encouraging self-reflection and course correction.


The problem is amplified by the scale of LLM deployment. Chatbots are instantly available


to millions of people, and their impact on human relationships can be enormous.


These built-in features of AI have clinical significance for vulnerable populations. People


who have psychiatric illnesses or overvalued ideas rely on reality testing through their


interaction with fellow human beings. A sycophantic AI chatbot may validate their


overvalued ideas instead of challenging them. This reduces the chance of critical feedback to


the human, resulting in worsening of symptoms. In such cases, LLMs are unlikely to be the


primary cause of psychosis. They may act as an amplifier of the preexisting psychopathology.


LLMs may even act as a coauthor in forming delusional narratives. The reality distortion thus


brought by the chatbot may affect treatment adherence in patients.


Though AI psychosis is widely used in media, it is not clinically validated. Studies have


shown that LLMs may become the subject of delusions. This was seen in earlier


technologies also. AI is described as a lover or God-like in clinical reports. This cannot be


classified as psychosis caused by AI. The vulnerable population throughout history has


incorporated the prevailing technology into their delusion. If this is the case, this represents


historical continuity rather than a clinical novelty.


Both product- and user-level factors can worsen psychotic symptoms. Product-level factors


include extended conversations, validating user beliefs, and simulating emotions and


personas. User-level factors include sleep deprivation, social isolation, and vulnerable


populations like psychotic patients, children, and adolescents.


Our information about AI and its role in psychosis is minimal. Data about the definition,


epidemiology, and whether LLMs are a causal factor are not clear. No prospective studies


have been done. We are not sure if excessive LLM use is a coping mechanism to deal with


stress or maladaptive, resulting in behavioural problems. This raises more questions, and only


long-term research about whether and how LLM use causes behavioural and brain changes


can identify vulnerable groups. What policy-level and engineering changes are needed to deal


with this situation will become important in the future.


More than two millennia ago, Socrates questioned the then-current technology of writing,


arguing that it weakened the human mind. However, over time, writing has transformed


human society in ways that no other technology has. It has even rewired the human brain.


Unlike writing, which preserves and transmits human knowledge, generative AI actively


participates in knowledge generation. Whether the current concerns regarding AI will prove


analogous to previous technological anxieties and whether LLMs ultimately reshape human


cognition through processes of both augmentation and cognitive surrender can only be


determined through longitudinal research. AI literacy, critical- thinking education, and


human-in-the-loop practices may help build resilience against cognitive surrender.
